    Cups around the world

    A “cup” is not a global unit. US recipes mean 236.588 mL (8 US fl oz). Australia and New Zealand mean a 250 mL metric cup. Much of Europe never uses a cup at all — grams and millilitres. Pick two places below. This page does not invent a Latin American cup or a single official Canadian cooking cup.

    Country comparator

    Side by side: typical liquid unit, typical dry-baking unit, what “1 cup” means if they use cups, pint size if it matters, and whether a scale is the norm. Default: United States vs Spain. Systems are never mixed silently.

    vs

    These two places follow the same kitchen convention for home cooking: metric grams and millilitres. Cups show up mainly when someone follows a US (or Australian) recipe.

    What other countries actually say

    United States

    Home recipes are written in US customary cups, tablespoons, and fluid ounces. 1 US customary cup = 8 US fl oz = 236.588 mL (from the US gallon of 231 cubic inches). Nutrition labels use a different legal cup of 240 mL (21 CFR 101.9) — not the kitchen cup. Baking is still often scooped, though a scale is better; King Arthur lists 1 US cup of all-purpose flour as 120 g.

    Canada

    Official measurement is metric. Canadian nutrition labelling uses a 250 mL cup. Home cooking is a mix: many newer recipes use millilitres and a 250 mL metric cup; plenty of cooks still follow US cups from US books and blogs; older books may use imperial fluid ounces. There is no single “official Canadian cooking cup” we can honestly pick — match the recipe’s source, or weigh.

    United Kingdom

    Modern British recipes are mostly grams and millilitres. A kitchen scale is the norm for baking. Historically, a British “cup” was often half an imperial pint: 10 imperial fl oz ≈ 284 mL. An imperial pint is 568 mL = 20 imperial fl oz, not 16 US fl oz. A US fluid ounce is 29.5735 mL; an imperial fluid ounce is 28.4130625 mL. Don’t swap pints.

    Australia

    The metric cup of 250 mL is standard in Australian recipes. Liquids are often given in millilitres as well. The Australian tablespoon is 20 mL (AS 1325), not the 15 mL / ½ US fl oz tablespoon. Cups remain common for home baking; grams appear too. Do not treat an Australian cup as 8 US fl oz.

    New Zealand

    Like Australia, recipes use a 250 mL metric cup. The New Zealand tablespoon is 15 mL (3 teaspoons), not Australia’s 20 mL. Edmonds and other NZ sources state the 250 mL cup outright. Grams are common; cups have not disappeared.

    Europe (Spain, France, Germany, Portugal)

    Home cooking is in grams and millilitres. Measuring cups marked in cups are uncommon except when following US blogs or imported gadgets. There is no Spanish, French, German, or Portuguese cooking cup to convert. If a Madrid kitchen meets a US cup of flour, weigh 120 g (King Arthur’s US-cup figure) rather than hunting a local cup.

    Japan

    Two sourced volumes, not one: a common Japanese kitchen measuring cup (1 カップ) is 200 mL. Rice, sake, and the little cup that comes with a rice cooker use the traditional gō (合) ≈ 180 mL. They are not a US cup. Modern cookbooks increasingly list grams. Do not use a 200 mL cup as 1 gō of rice.

    Latin America

    Official units are metric (mL, grams, litres). There is no “Latin cup” to quote. Many home cooks still use US cups because so many recipes arrive from the United States. Treat that mix as a mix: if the recipe is US, use 236.588 mL; if it lists millilitres or grams, use those. Don’t average them.

    Gotchas

    • Never mix systems in one recipe. A US cup (236.588 mL), a metric cup (250 mL), a Japanese 200 mL cup, and a historical imperial cup (~284 mL) share a word, not a size.
    • Pints are worse. US pint = 16 US fl oz ≈ 473 mL. Imperial pint = 20 imperial fl oz ≈ 568 mL. A UK pub pint is not a US measuring pint.
    • Nutrition-label cups are legal definitions, not kitchen cups. US labels: 240 mL. Canadian labels: 250 mL. Neither is an excuse to rewrite a recipe that was tested in the other cup.
    • Flour grams belong to a specific cup. 120 g is King Arthur’s figure for 1 US customary cup of all-purpose flour. It is not the weight of a 250 mL or 200 mL cup of flour.

    FAQ

    Why isn’t 1 cup the same everywhere?

    Because “cup” is a kitchen convention, not a single SI unit. The US customary cup is 236.588 mL (8 US fl oz). Australia and New Zealand use 250 mL. A historical British cup was about 284 mL. Japan’s common measuring cup is 200 mL, and a gō is about 180 mL. Much of Europe does not use a cup.

    I am in Spain. Which cup should I use?

    Spanish recipes use grams and millilitres, not cups. If you are following a US recipe, use 236.588 mL (or weigh flour at 120 g per US cup of all-purpose, King Arthur). Do not assume a 250 mL metric cup unless the recipe is Australian, New Zealand, or another metric-cup source.

    Is a UK pint 16 ounces?

    No. An imperial pint is 20 imperial fluid ounces (568 mL). A US pint is 16 US fluid ounces (473 mL). The fluid ounces themselves also differ (29.5735 mL US vs 28.4130625 mL imperial).

    Does Japan use a 200 mL cup or 180 mL?

    Both, for different jobs. A common Japanese kitchen measuring cup (カップ) is 200 mL. Rice, sake, and the rice-cooker cup use the gō, about 180 mL. They are not interchangeable with a US cup.
